<p>This <span style="text-decoration: line-through;">article</span> <em><a href="https://www.mcall.com/news/all-whiteboxnw5sf6orik89.6980311aug07,0,4919882.column">editorial</a> (original behind paywall) </em>(via this <a href="https://blogs.edweek.org/edweek/speced/2009/08/district_sues_parent_for_frivo_1.html">post</a>) from Pennsylvania describes one of every special education parent&#8217;s many fears about the due process procedure: having to pay the school district&#8217;s legal fees if they lose.</p>
<p>Reading the story, and the complaint (<a href="{{ site.baseurl }}/?attachment_id=351">PDF</a>) , it seems clear that there are three main issues:</p>
<ol>
<li> The parent has made a large number of due process hearing, mediation, and OCR requests and complaints. (Note: there&#8217;s no real way to tell from the complaint to what extent these requests were in good faith.)</li>
<li> The parent appears to have lost all the due process hearings, mediation did not work, and OCR dismissed the complaints. (Note: this is not &#8220;proof&#8221; but  may be evidence that the parent&#8217;s actions were not in good faith.)</li>
<li> The parent allegedly made a statement to the mediator about a desire to use dispute resolution processes to increase costs for the district, theoretically leading to a decision to accede to the parent&#8217;s demands.</li>
</ol>
<p>What are the lessons? First, don&#8217;t use procedures for an improper purpose. It probably won&#8217;t really help in any case. Second, if you are thinking that way, or even just blowing off steam or wondering whether a district makes that sort of cost to fight vs. cost to serve analysis, keep it to yourself. Mediators, hearing officers, and judges all have some measure of responsibility to ensuring a fair system for all. Even your own lawyer has a responsibility to protect the system from abuse (school district lawyers have the same responsibility, but there&#8217;s no specific part of IDEA that makes them personally liable for maintaining an improper action like there is for parents&#8217; lawyers).</p>
<p>Think of it this way: if a special education director told a mediator something like this: &#8220;Parent&#8217;s proposal is appropriate, but it will be cheaper for us to just fight her rather than provide services to her son,&#8221; wouldn&#8217;t you be appropriately outraged? Isn&#8217;t that the sort of frivolous use of due process that would drive you up the wall? Wouldn&#8217;t you expect a judge to at least hear your complaint and look into the matter? I know I would.</p>
<p>Have district employees told, or warned, you about having to pay school district lawyers&#8217; fees? My sense is that the language used is probably overbearing and more than a little slanted in many cases. Your best way as parents to protect yourselves is to get competent advice when you&#8217;re considering due process or related steps. Plenty of lawyers and advocates like myself are available to give advice in many different ways: <a href="{{ site.baseurl }}/">blogs</a>, presentations, <a href="{{ site.baseurl }}/roving-office-hours/">Q&amp;A sessions</a>, or even <a href="https://twitter.com/rickcolosimo">twitter</a>.</p>
<p>And, of course, don&#8217;t forget the groups of parents supporting parents where we can learn from each other and help everyone&#8217;s kids.</p>
