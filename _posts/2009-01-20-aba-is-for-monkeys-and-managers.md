<p>I recently puchased the book &#8220;<a href="https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/1400064287?ie=UTF8&amp;tag=thoughtstorm&amp;linkCode=as2&amp;camp=1789&amp;creative=390957&amp;creativeASIN=1400064287">Made to Stick</a>,&#8221; and I&#8217;ve spent a little time catching up on the authors&#8217; articles in <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/">Fast Company</a> magazine. <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/756459/your-boss-monkey">One</a> caught my eye and seemed eerily familiar.</p>
<p>This article describes, of all things, using some standard ABA techniques to &#8220;handle&#8221; a difficult boss. It mentions the notion that everything you do can reinforce bad behavior, even things that we wouldn&#8217;t typically find reinforcing.</p>
<p>Here are the three &#8220;rules&#8221; from the article. How many of them do you use with your autistic child?</p>
<ol>
<li>Ignore bad behavior.</li>
<li>Every response is potentially reinforcing.</li>
<li>Reward desired behavior.</li>
</ol>
<p>At first, I wasn&#8217;t sure that I was going to write about this article, but it doesn&#8217;t actually compare our kids to animals, any more than our therapist is calling her husband an animal when she talks about using ABA to keep him on the &#8220;straight and narrow.&#8221; After all, behavior in this context is mostly about reinforcement for desired behavior.</p>
<p>As my <a href="https://webpub.allegheny.edu/employee/T/tnonnenm/">economist</a> friend would say, if you choose B over A, it&#8217;s because you actually want B more &#8212; you get more from B. If you&#8217;re happier, who cares if someone is deliberately trying to make you happier? Isn&#8217;t that a good thing? The world would be a better place if we all spent a little time deliberately trying to make other people happier. I know it&#8217;s true for our son and for our family.</p>
